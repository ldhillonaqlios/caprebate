If your name is in a document someone gave us

Cap Rebate (CAP REBATE LTD) works out how much tax relief a business can claim on the fittings and equipment in a building. To do that we are given documents about the building by the client who instructs us — for example a lease, a sale contract, replies to enquiries made when a property was sold, or a certificate for building works.

Those documents often name people who are not our client — a landlord or a tenant, someone who sold the property, someone who witnessed a signature, or the surveyor or engineer who produced a report. If you are one of those people, this notice is for you, and it is here because we could not find an address to write to you at.

Where your information came from

We were given it by our client — the buyer, the tenant or the owner of the property — in a document about that property. It is not a public document and it did not come from a public source. We did not obtain it from you.

What we hold

Whatever the document itself says about you. Typically that is your name and the capacity you appear in — a party to a contract or a lease, a witness to a signature, or the author of a report — together with any address or description the document gives for you.

If you would like to know exactly what the document says about you, write to us and tell us the property or the transaction, and we will tell you. That is also how we identify you: for this kind of record we usually cannot look you up from a name alone, so what you tell us about the property is what lets us find the entry.

What we use it for, and why we are allowed to

Our client is claiming tax relief on the fittings in a building. The rules limit what they can claim by reference to what earlier owners could have claimed, and to what was agreed about the fittings when the property changed hands. So we have to establish who the parties were and what was agreed. Your name is in the document that records that.

The names rule

We strip people's names out of these records at the point we receive them. An EPC assessor's name, a care home's registered manager, a charity's trustees — these arrive in the record because the register cannot be queried for the building facts alone, but the name is dropped before it is stored. There are two exceptions, and only two:

  1. Where a company's controlling owner is recorded at Companies House. The Annual Investment Allowance is shared between businesses under the same control. If a person controls more than one business, the allowance that can be claimed is shared across them, not multiplied. We have to be able to check who controls what — otherwise a claim could be sized too generously and come back on our client when HMRC checks it. We only check companies our client has already told us about; we do not search the register to find out what else they might control.
  2. Where the property is owned in someone's own name rather than by a company. If a surgery or care home is owned personally rather than through a company, the person named on the title is the person making the claim. We have to record who that is, because the claim belongs to them.

What we never take

We do not take the names of people connected with planning applications — applicants, agents, objectors or neighbours — from planning records, under any circumstances. Planning records can carry very personal information (a health condition mentioned in an objection, a neighbour dispute, a family circumstance) that has nothing to do with a building's tax treatment. We use planning records only for the application reference, date, description and decision. This is the strongest protection on this page, and we mean it to stay that way.

Some records from these registers are retrieved in full and the names in them are discarded at the point we receive them, before anything is stored. We are telling you that because the retrieval is still something we did with your information, even where nothing was kept.

How long we keep it

We keep evidence supporting a claim until the later of: the point at which HMRC can no longer open an enquiry into the tax return the claim was made in, and the point at which any enquiry actually opened has closed — plus a margin. If your name reached us from a public register and you are not our client

This section is written to you directly.

You are not our client. You may be the controlling owner of a company that shares common control with a business we have been engaged to work on — for example, you and our client jointly control two related companies, or you are a business partner, co-shareholder or family member recorded at Companies House. Your name reached us because we had to check whether the Annual Investment Allowance is shared between your business and our client's.

Why we hold your name. To confirm whether a business you control shares common control with a business our client has instructed us on, so that we size our client's Annual Investment Allowance claim correctly.

Our lawful basis. Legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)). We rely on this because checking common control protects our client from an over-claimed allowance and protects the public interest in tax reliefs being claimed correctly — not because it is convenient for us. We do not search the register to find out what you control; we only confirm companies our client has already named to us.

What we hold. Your name, the month and year of your birth, and your nationality, as published by Companies House. We do not hold your date of birth (Companies House does not publish it), your home address, or your officer or director details from any other Companies House record — we never request that data.

How to ask what we hold about you. Write to us using the contact details above. We will respond without undue delay and in any event before the end of the applicable time period — normally one month beginning with the day we receive your request (Articles 12(3) and 12A(1) of the UK GDPR).

If you are not satisfied

You have three separate options, and you do not have to use them in order.

1. Complain to us. You have a right to complain to us about how we have handled your personal data — section 164A of the Data Protection Act 2018. We will confirm we have received it within 30 days, look into it, and tell you the outcome. There is a form above, and you can also just write to us.

2. Complain to the data protection regulator. You have a right to complain to the Information Commissioner (from 30 September 2026, the Information Commission) — section 165 of the Data Protection Act 2018. You do not have to complain to us first, and you do not have to wait for us to finish. The regulator must look into it to the extent appropriate, keep you informed of progress, tell you the outcome, and tell you about your rights under section 166 of that Act — which let you apply to the Tribunal if the regulator does not deal with your complaint. The regulator's contact details are at ico.org.uk, and the helpline is 0303 123 1113.

3. Go to court. You can also take your own case to court. A court can order us to do something, or to stop doing something (section 167 of the Data Protection Act 2018), and can award compensation — which can include compensation for distress (section 168 of that Act, with Article 82 of the UK GDPR). This is your right and it does not depend on what we or the regulator decide.

A complaint about our service is a different thing. If your complaint is about our work, our fee, delay or the conduct of someone acting for us, rather than about your personal data, it is handled under our separate complaints policy, which we will send you on request. If your complaint is about both, we deal with both — and the timescales above still apply to the personal data part.

We would like to use Google Analytics to count how people move through our free assessment, so that we can make it clearer. It is off unless you say yes. When you first visit, our website asks you, with a Yes and a No of the same size. Nothing that counts your visit is loaded until you press Yes. If you say yes, Google Analytics sets cookies whose names begin with _ga. They let Google tell one visit from the next for up to two years. We never send your name, your business’s name, your address, your postcode or any figure. We turn off Google’s advertising features and its linking of visits across devices.
