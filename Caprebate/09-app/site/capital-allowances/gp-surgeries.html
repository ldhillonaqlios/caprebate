Can a GP surgery claim capital allowances?

The short answer

Usually, yes. Capital allowances for GP surgeries cover the fixtures built into the premises, such as hand basins, heating, lighting, data cabling and fire alarms. Partners who own the building can claim, and a practice that leases can usually claim on the fit-out it paid for. Any part an NHS grant paid for is left out.

It is a tax relief on the practice's profits, not a grant. How much is open depends on how you came to own the surgery, what you spent and what has been claimed on it before.

Key points

  • Built-in fixtures can qualify. Loose equipment is usually claimed by your accountant already.
  • Partner-owned surgery: the partners claim. Leased, LIFT, NHS Property Services or pension-owned: the practice claims its own fit-out.
  • An NHS improvement grant and a tax allowance cannot cover the same part of the works.
  • Notional rent is income, not a grant. It does not reduce your claim.
  • Past spending can often still be claimed if the items are still owned and nobody has claimed them.
  • A flat 3% fee, a free assessment, and a chartered surveyor signs every split of costs.

What can shrink a GP surgery claim

Few things stop a GP claim outright. More often, something reduces it. These are the points we check first.

General notes6 of 6

  1. [01]

    NHS improvement grants

    In England, a grant cannot pay for any part of the works on which you also claim a tax allowance. We set the grant-funded parts aside first. If the grant names specific works, such as heating or electrics, those items come out in full.

  2. [02]

    What you told the NHS

    A practice states its tax allowance plans when it applies for a grant. Claiming on a part the grant paid for can put the grant at risk. If a grant is later repaid, the allowances set aside do not come back.

  3. [03]

    Flats and converted houses

    The rules that limit claims on rented homes do not apply to the surgery itself. If part of the building is a flat or a home, that part is taken out.

  4. [04]

    VAT you cannot reclaim

    Medical services are largely exempt from VAT, so VAT the practice cannot reclaim is part of its cost and can count towards the claim. Dispensing practices need a closer look.

  5. [05]

    Claims made before

    Fixtures can only be claimed once. If only loose equipment was claimed, such as furniture, fridges and IT, the built-in fixtures may still be open. Practices using the cash basis cannot claim on fixtures.

  6. [06]

    Practices in Wales

    The grant rules above are England's. Wales has its own premises funding rules, and we check them before relying on any figure.

A guide to capital allowances for GP surgeries

For GP partners, practice managers and their accountants weighing up primary care premises capital allowances on a surgery, medical centre or health centre.

01 · The basicsWhat are capital allowances for GP surgeries?

Capital allowances are a tax relief on money a business spends on plant and machinery. In a GP surgery, much of that plant is built into the building: the hand basins and clinical plumbing, the heating, the wiring and lighting, the data cabling and the fire alarm. These items are called fixtures. They can qualify even though they are part of the building.

Running a GP practice is a profession, so the claim reduces the tax paid on the practice's profits. For partners that is income tax. For an incorporated practice it is corporation tax. The tax saved depends on each claimant's own tax rate.

Worth knowing

Loose equipment, such as examination couches, free-standing vaccine fridges and computers, is usually claimed by your accountant already. A specialist claim looks at the fixtures built into the building. We never claim the same item twice.

02 · What qualifiesWhat qualifies in a GP surgery or medical centre?

A modern purpose-built medical centre usually holds a lot of qualifying fixtures. It has many consulting rooms, each with clinical plumbing, lighting and data points, and sometimes a minor ops room or dispensary. A converted house or high-street surgery usually holds fewer.

AreaCan qualifyUsually not in this claim
Consulting roomsClinical hand basins, dedicated plumbing, fixed examination lights, fitted worktopsCouches and portable kit
Building servicesHeating, hot and cold water, wiring, lighting, ventilation, liftsWalls, floors, roof and the land
ReceptionFitted reception desk, check-in points, patient call screens, phone systemsFree-standing furniture and computers
Safety and securityFire alarm, CCTV, intruder alarm, door entryItems you or a past owner already claimed
Specialist areasMinor ops fittings, dispensary fit-out, power and cooling for vaccine storage, hoistsFree-standing vaccine fridges

Some items depend on why they are there. Soundproofing, for example, may qualify where it is needed to keep consultations private, but not where it only makes the rooms more comfortable. The surveyor checks each one. You can see where the main items sit in the section through a GP surgery above.

If part of the building is a flat or a home, that part is taken out. The rest of the surgery is not affected, because the rules that limit claims on rented homes do not apply to a GP practice.

03 · Who claimsWho claims capital allowances in a GP partnership?

The claim belongs to whoever carries on the practice and paid for the work, or to the partners who own the building. That can be:

  • a GP partnership or LLP;
  • an incorporated practice (a limited company);
  • a federation or primary care network (PCN) body that pays tax;
  • the GP partners who own the premises themselves.

Salaried GPs and locums are not the claimant. The claimant must also pay UK income tax or corporation tax and keep its accounts on the normal (accruals) basis. Practices using the cash basis cannot claim on building fixtures.

Structure changes some details. A few faster allowances for new equipment are open only to companies. Where several practices are under common control, they may have to share one Annual Investment Allowance. That affects how fast the relief arrives, not whether it is available.

04 · Leases and fit-outsCan a practice claim if it leases its surgery?

Who owns the building usually decides who can claim.

Who owns the buildingWho usually claims
The GP partnersThe partners, on the building's fixtures
A private landlordThe practice, on the fit-out it paid for
A developer, LIFT, PFI or REIT ownerThe practice, on its own fit-out. These owners usually cannot claim.
NHS Property Services or another NHS bodyThe practice, on its own fit-out. NHS bodies do not pay tax.
A pension scheme (SIPP or SSAS)The practice, on its own fit-out. The scheme does not pay tax.

A practice can claim on the fit-out it pays for, even on a short lease. Two things can reduce it. If the landlord or the NHS paid towards the fit-out, your claim goes down by that amount, and the contributor may be able to claim that part instead. And lease wording that is silent or unclear can leave neither side able to claim, so we read the lease before relying on any figure.

The fit-out rules work in much the same way for capital allowances for dental practices, which are often leased too.

05 · BuyingBuying a surgery, or buying into the partnership property

When you buy a surgery, part of the price pays for the fixtures in it. The claim comes from splitting the price fairly between the land, the structure and the fixtures. A chartered surveyor makes and signs that split.

Who sold it to you matters most. An NHS body, NHS Property Services, a developer or a pension scheme could not claim, so a fresh claim may be open to you. If the seller could claim, your claim may be limited by what they claimed and by any value fixed in a capital allowances election made at the sale. If the seller owned the building from before April 2008, some features such as heating and electrical systems could not be claimed then, which may leave more open to you.

The paperwork helps: the sale contract, the pre-contract enquiry replies (CPSEs) and any election. As the buyer, it is for you to show what you are entitled to. Gaps are a reason to look further, not to stop.

When a partner buys into the premises

NHS general practice goodwill cannot be sold. So when a partner joins or retires, what usually changes hands is a share of the building. That may open a claim, or it may not. It depends on who owns the premises now, how the share passes and whether anyone has claimed before. Transfers between partners can fall under special rules for connected people, so a specialist checks the history before any figure is relied on.

06 · Building workExtensions, refurbishments and repairs at a GP surgery

Practices extend and refurbish often, for more capacity, for CQC standards or for infection control. New consulting rooms bring new plumbing, heating, lighting and cabling, which can qualify. We build these claims from the invoices and the contractor's final account.

On a new build or extension, the structure itself (walls, floors and roof) is not plant. It may get a separate, slower relief called the structures and buildings allowance.

A refurbishment needs a split between improvements and repairs:

  • Improvements, such as adding consulting rooms or changing the layout, are usually capital. The fixtures in them can qualify for allowances.
  • Like-for-like repairs can often be deducted in full from profits straight away instead.
  • Run-down premises bought and put right before you moved in are a trap. That work is usually capital, not repairs.
  • Replacing most of a system, such as the heating or the wiring, is usually treated as capital.

Professional fees can usually be spread across the qualifying items. Only the part the practice actually paid counts. If the NHS paid back some of the fees, that part is left out.

07 · NHS grantsHow do NHS improvement grants affect capital allowances?

This is the biggest single point in most GP claims. In England, the NHS premises rules say an improvement grant cannot pay for any part of the works on which a tax allowance is claimed. So we set the grant-funded parts aside before we work out the claim. Other public money towards the works, such as developer contributions or modernisation funds, is treated the same way.

How the grant comes out depends on the offer letter. If it names specific works, such as heating or electrics, those items come out in full. If it is a share of the whole scheme cost, the claim is reduced in proportion. So we read the letter rather than assume a simple share.

Worth knowing

When a practice applies for a grant, it states its plans for tax allowances. Claiming on a part the grant paid for can put the grant itself at risk. And if a grant is later repaid, for example because the building stops being used as a surgery within the agreed period, the allowances set aside do not come back.

These grant rules are England's. Wales has its own premises funding rules, and we check them before relying on any figure. See also what can shrink a GP surgery claim.

08 · Notional rentNotional rent, and choosing between a grant and self-funding

Notional rent and borrowing-cost reimbursement are income, not a capital grant. They do not reduce the spending you can claim on. A practice that owns its premises receives one or the other, not both.

Taking a grant does reduce (abate) the practice's notional rent for a set period, and the period is longer for larger grants. So for a planned project, choosing between a grant and funding the work yourself is a real decision.

PointNHS improvement grantSelf-fund and claim allowances
Upfront costNone for the part the grant coversYou fund or borrow it
Notional rentReduced for a set periodKept in full
ConditionsA guaranteed-use period, possibly a legal charge, and repayment if the contract ends earlyFree to use or sell the premises
Tax reliefNone on the grant-funded partsCapital allowances on the qualifying items

Which is better depends on your numbers. A review before the funding decision adds the most value. We can model both routes with you, so you decide with the figures in front of you.

09 · TimingCan a GP practice claim capital allowances for past years?

Often, yes. If the partners or the practice still own the items and nobody has claimed on them, past spending may still carry a claim. That includes a surgery bought years ago or consulting rooms added in an earlier extension.

Timing usually changes the speed of the relief, not the total. If the relevant tax return can still be amended, faster relief such as the Annual Investment Allowance, and even a refund, may be possible. If it cannot, the relief is not lost. It comes through more slowly in later returns.

Fixtures can only be claimed once. If your accountant has claimed only loose equipment, the built-in fixtures may still be open. That is why we ask whether anyone has claimed on the building before.

10 · Evidence and costHow a GP surgery claim is evidenced, and what it costs

A chartered surveyor makes and signs every split of costs. On a purchase, that is a fair split of the price. On building work, it is an analysis of the invoices. You and your accountant get a report with the figures for your tax return and the evidence behind them.

We work alongside your accountant, who puts the claim into the practice's or the partners' returns. If HMRC asks questions, we support our valuation and cost split free of charge, with no time limit. It does not cover tribunal representation.

Our fee is a flat 3% of the capital allowances we identify. The assessment is free. If there is no claim, we tell you early, in writing. A pension scheme such as a SIPP or SSAS does not pay tax, so it cannot claim. The practice may still claim on the fit-out it paid for.

No. Notional rent and borrowing-cost reimbursement are income, not a capital grant. They do not reduce the spending you can claim on.

It can. GP goodwill cannot be sold, so what changes hands is usually the building. We look at who owns it now, what was paid and whether anyone has claimed on it before.

Note 06Should we take a grant or fund the work ourselves?

It depends on your numbers. A grant reduces your notional rent and comes with conditions. Funding the work yourself keeps your full notional rent and the tax relief. We can look at both with you before you decide.

Often, yes, if the items are still owned and nobody has claimed on them. If the tax return can still be amended, relief can come faster and may give a refund. If not, the relief is not lost. It comes through more slowly in later returns.
