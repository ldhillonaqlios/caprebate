Can supported living claim capital allowances?

The short answer

It depends on the building, and the honest answer can be nil. Capital allowances for supported living never reach the homes let to tenants, or anything inside them. In a building with two or more homes, the shared stairs, lift, corridors, staff areas and landlord's services outside the homes can qualify, if their cost can be split out.

Shared kitchens and lounges that are part of residents' homes do not count, and nor does a boiler that heats the homes. If our free assessment finds no claim, we tell you early, in writing.

Key points

  • Homes let to tenants, and everything inside them, do not qualify.
  • A single house or flat let as one home usually has no claim at all.
  • Shared kitchens and lounges count as part of the residents' homes.
  • Plant that heats the homes is left out, even in a shared plant room.
  • Grant-funded spend comes out, and charities usually cannot claim.
  • The assessment is free. Our fee is a flat 3% of the allowances we identify.

In a building with two or more homes, the parts outside the homes can carry a claim: the shared core, the staff areas and the landlord's services, each costed separately.

A guide to capital allowances for supported living and supported housing

For investors, owners and providers of supported living and specialist supported housing in England and Wales, and for their accountants.

01 · The basicsWhat are capital allowances for supported living?

Capital allowances are a tax relief on some kinds of capital spending. For property, that mostly means the fixtures and systems inside a building, such as lighting, lifts, fire alarms and door entry. The allowances reduce the income tax or corporation tax on your profits. They are not a capital gains tax relief, and they only help if there is a tax bill to reduce.

In most healthcare property, such as private hospitals and clinics, the fixtures across the whole building can be looked at. Supported living is different. Residents hold their own tenancies and live in their own homes, and the tax rules shut homes out of a landlord's claim. So capital allowances for supported living usually reach only part of the building, and sometimes none of it.

Worth knowing

This is the one sector where we would rather under-promise. Supported living is often sold to investors on tax benefits that do not survive the rules on homes.

02 · Care home or supported living?Is supported living taxed like a care home?

Not usually, and this is the first thing we settle. Two buildings can look the same and be taxed in opposite ways. What matters is how people live there.

How people live thereHow it is usually treated
Residents have their own tenancy and live there as their home, with support provided as a serviceSupported living. The homes are left out, and any claim is limited to the shared parts.
Residents live in a registered care home, with no tenancy of their ownA care home. The building is not treated as homes, so a fuller claim may be possible.
Not sure, or a mix of bothWe take the narrower view until a specialist has checked the facts.

Registration helps: a registered provider letting homes points to supported living, while a registered care home points the other way. Extra-care and housing with care for older people follow the same test: where residents hold tenancies, their flats are homes. If your building turns out to be a care home, read our guide to capital allowances for care homes.

A gap between tenancies changes nothing. A building empty partway through conversion from another use, such as a former care home, needs a specialist look.

03 · Homes and common partsWhich parts of a supported housing scheme can qualify?

The answer turns on how many homes there are, and whether each one has its own kitchen and bathroom.

How the homes are laid outWhat we can look at
One house or flat, let as one homeUsually nothing. The whole property is a home from end to end.
Several self-contained flats, each with its own kitchen and bathroomThe shared parts outside the flats: entrance, corridors, stairs, lift, staff rooms and the landlord's services.
Several bedrooms that share a kitchen or lounge, as in a shared houseThe same shared parts. The shared kitchen and lounge count as part of the residents' homes and are left out.

The same reasoning applies to the communal areas of a house in multiple occupation (HMO). Where a resident's own room does not give them everything they need for daily life, the shared kitchen and lounge complete their home. Those rooms are part of the homes, so they do not qualify. Hallways, stairs, a lift and the systems that serve them are different, because nobody lives in them.

One point is not settled. Where flats are fully self-contained and there is also a separate shared lounge, it is not clear whether that lounge is a common part or part of the homes. We leave it out until that is confirmed, so it could only ever add to a figure later.

04 · What usually qualifiesCapital allowances on common parts: what can qualify?

In a building with two or more homes, these items can qualify where they sit in, or serve only, the shared parts:

  • the landlord's electrical supply, and corridor, stair and emergency lighting
  • a lift serving the shared parts
  • the fire alarm, with its detectors and panel in corridors and stairs
  • door entry, access control and CCTV
  • fittings in a staff office, staff WC or sleep-in room, including a staff kitchen, fitted furniture and data cabling
  • staff call systems, and hoists or accessible bathing in shared or staff areas, never inside a home
  • switchgear, and any plant that serves only the shared parts
  • building work needed to install those items, such as forming an opening for a lift

Being on a list is not the same as qualifying. For some items, such as a fire alarm or a staff kitchen, we still have to show that each one is equipment used in the business. We check item by item, and leave out anything we cannot show.

Where one system serves both the homes and the shared parts, such as a main electrical supply, its cost is split between the two, and only the shared part counts.

Does a communal boiler or plant room qualify?

A shared plant room is not claimable just because it sits outside the homes. What matters is what the equipment serves, not where it stands. A communal boiler, heat pump or district heating system that heats the homes or makes their hot water is left out in full, even in a shared plant room or outside the building. So is the building work to house it. It is often the largest single item.

05 · Splitting the costsWhy the cost of the common parts must be split out

Shared parts only count if their cost can be separately identified from the cost of the homes. In one tribunal case about shared houses, the landlord was right that some common parts could qualify, but lost the claim because the costs had never been split out.

The best evidence is a cost plan or final account that shows the shared areas on their own, with floor plans marking the homes, the shared parts and the staff rooms. Where costs were only ever recorded as one blended figure, a chartered surveyor can sometimes split them afterwards from the plans and cost records. A chartered surveyor signs every split of costs we produce.

If the cost truly cannot be split, there is no claim we would stand behind, and we will say so rather than guess.

06 · Who can claimSpecialist supported housing: who can claim?

Specialist supported housing is often run on a lease model. An investor owns the building and leases it to a registered provider, which lets the homes to tenants. The council arranges care separately.

Who you areYour likely position
Investor or owner that lets the buildingYou run a letting business, which is what the rules on homes target. Any claim is limited to the shared parts you paid for. You are often the only taxpayer in the chain.
Registered provider or housing association that leases and sub-letsUsually no spend of its own to claim, and often a charity or other body that does not pay this tax.
Care or support providerYou run a care business, not a letting business, but you usually do not own the building. Any claim is on fit-out and equipment you paid for yourself, such as a staff office, and used in your business.

Capital allowances reduce a tax bill, so the claimant must pay UK income tax or corporation tax. Charities, CICs and many housing associations do not, so they cannot claim. A sole trader or partnership using the cash basis cannot claim on property fixtures either.

If you are weighing up supported living as an investment, treat any promised tax benefit with care. The claim is usually small, and sometimes nil.

07 · Buying a schemeBuying a supported living property: what the seller claimed matters

If you bought an existing scheme, the seller's history shapes your claim. We check:

  • whether the seller, or an earlier owner, claimed on the shared-area fixtures
  • whether you and the seller agreed a fixed value for the fixtures in the sale, and what it was
  • whether the seller could claim at all. If the seller was a charity or another body that does not pay this tax, a fresh claim on the shared parts may be open to you
  • whether the sale was between connected companies, which needs a specialist look
  • the seller's replies to enquiries on capital allowances

An agreed fixed value can cap your claim, but it never widens it. Even if the value covers the whole building, your claim still reaches only the shared parts. As the buyer, you have to prove the claim, which is why the paperwork from the sale matters.

Where the seller bought the building before April 2008, some systems in the shared parts may be open to a claim the seller could not have made. We check this on the facts.

08 · Building and refurbishingBuilding, converting or refurbishing supported housing

For a new build or conversion, we work straight from the build costs of the shared and staff areas. A fair share of professional fees and site costs can follow the qualifying items, but only their share.

For a refurbishment, new systems in corridors, stairs or staff areas may qualify. Like-for-like repairs in the shared parts may instead be deducted in full as a repair. Work inside the homes is outside the claim either way.

VAT you cannot reclaim can form part of the cost. New-build construction of homes is often zero-rated, though, so there may be little VAT to include.

Timing matters too. If the tax return for the year of spend can still be amended, the Annual Investment Allowance may relieve the qualifying spend in full in that year. If not, relief usually comes more slowly, through writing-down allowances.

09 · Grants and other reliefsGrant funding, and other supported living property tax relief

Supported housing is often part-funded by capital grants, such as Homes England, GLA or council funding. Spend paid for by a grant cannot be claimed, so it comes out first. That can shrink a small claim further. Repaying the grant later, for example on an early sale or a change of use, does not bring the relief back.

When capital allowances come to little or nothing, other reliefs may still help:

  • Land Remediation Relief. A company that bought a derelict or contaminated building, and did not cause the problem, may get an extra deduction for cleaning it up, for example removing asbestos. This relief is not limited by the rules on homes, so on a conversion it can be worth more than capital allowances.
  • Replacement of Domestic Items Relief. This covers replacing items inside let homes. It is not a capital allowance, but your accountant can look at it.

The structures and buildings allowance is generally not available for homes either.

Questions about supported living claims

Note 01Do supported living flats qualify for capital allowances?

No. Flats let to tenants are homes, and the rules exclude them and everything inside them. In a building with two or more homes, the shared and staff areas outside the homes can qualify, if their cost can be separately identified.

Note 02Is supported living taxed like a care home?

Not usually. Where residents have their own tenancy, any claim is limited to the shared parts. Where they live in a registered care home with no tenancy of their own, the building is not treated as homes and a fuller claim may be possible.

Note 03Can a housing association or charity claim?

Usually not. Capital allowances reduce a tax bill, and charities, CICs and many housing associations do not pay this tax. In a lease-based scheme, the investor that owns the building may be the one with a claim.

Note 04Does a communal boiler qualify?

Not where it heats the homes. Plant that serves the homes is excluded wherever it stands. Plant that serves only the shared areas can qualify.

Note 05Part of the build was grant-funded. Can we still claim?

Only on the part you paid for yourself. Spend funded by a capital grant is taken out first. Repaying the grant later, for example on an early sale, does not bring the relief back.

Note 06Is there any other relief if capital allowances come to nil?

Sometimes. A company that bought a derelict or contaminated building, and did not cause the problem, may be able to claim Land Remediation Relief. Items inside the homes may fall under Replacement of Domestic Items Relief, which your accountant can look at.

Note 07Can I claim capital allowances on the communal areas of an HMO or shared house?

Only on the parts with no living function. Where residents share a kitchen or lounge because their own room lacks one, that space is part of their homes and does not qualify. Hallways, stairs, a lift, the fire alarm and door entry outside the homes can, if their cost is split out.

Note 08Can an investor in specialist supported housing claim capital allowances?

Sometimes, but the claim is usually small. An investor that owns the building and leases it to a registered provider runs a letting business, so the homes are left out. Any claim is on the shared parts it paid for, after any grant is taken out.

Note 09What if the cost of the shared areas was never split out?

One blended figure can sink a claim, even where the items would qualify. A chartered surveyor can sometimes split the cost afterwards from plans and cost records. If that is not possible, we will tell you there is no claim we can stand behind.

Note 10What does a supported living claim cost?

The assessment is free. If there is a claim worth making, our fee is a flat 3% of the capital allowances we identify. If there is no claim, we tell you early, in writing.
