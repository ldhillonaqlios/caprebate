## The honest position, and it is why this is a short engagement rather than a long one

 **We built everything and we filled in nothing.**

 Every cell in every one of these documents that calls for a surveyor's judgement is **empty**, on purpose. Not one coefficient has a value. Not one qualifying percentage has been pre-filled. Not one pool allocation on a contested item has been resolved. Where a straw-man document previously left a blank with nothing beside it, we replaced the blank with **the question it was standing in for** — because a blank reads as an oversight and a question reads as a decision point.

## SV-S1 · How floor area and a room list become countable quantities

|  **What it is** |  The model that turns what we know about a building — its floor area, its room schedule, its bed count — into the quantities a valuation needs. Fifteen coefficients, each one the link between a fact we can establish and a number of things in a building. |
| --- | --- |
|  **What state it is in** |  **Complete draft, second version, awaiting your sign-off. No coefficient has a value.** Every blank at version one is still blank. What changed is **which question each blank asks**. |
|  **The finding that produced version two, and it is why we want you rather than a better number** |  One coefficient was specified as "fire-detection points per square metre". Tested against a real 268 m² dental fit-out: **the area method gives 2.4 to 4.8 detection points; a room-by-room count gives 15 to 19.** That is not a factor-of-two disagreement a better coefficient would close. **Detection follows compartmentation, not floor area** — a 14 m² surgery needs its own detector whether the building around it is 268 m² or 2,680 m² — and healthcare buildings are heavily cellularised. An area density derived from spacing implicitly assumes an open floor plate. **And it generalises**: any device whose real driver is one per enclosed space has the same defect if modelled per square metre. |
|  **The rule we adopted, which is the thing to confirm or overturn** |  **A quantity whose real driver is the number of enclosed spaces is derived room by room. An area-density figure for the same quantity is kept only as a labelled sanity floor — a lower bound saying "fewer than this would be surprising" — and may never be the number that reaches a report.** The area readings were not deleted, because deleting them loses a genuine check. They were demoted, and **the model refuses to emit one as a result**: there is no code path by which an area density becomes an answer. |
|  **What we are asking you to do** |  Five questions, and **the first two are the ones to answer first.** **(a)** How is a theatre suite counted? **(b)** Is 15 to 19 the right order of magnitude for detection in a 268 m² dental practice — and how do you count corridors, and do you count voids above suspended ceilings? **(c)** Which other quantities have we modelled against the wrong driver — sprinkler heads, emergency lighting, data outlets, CCTV points, hand-wash basins, medical gas outlets: area, rooms, beds, or something else? **And where a rule of thumb is genuinely fine, say so — we do not want to over-engineer this.** **(d)** What proportion of care-home bedrooms are typically hoist-fitted? **If there is no typical figure, "there is no such number" is as useful to us as a number.** **(e)** Where should these coefficients come from at all? We assumed real completed projects per sector rather than a published national average, on the grounds that density varies with acuity. Is that right? |
|  **If you confirm** |  The coefficients get values and the model starts producing candidate quantities for you to confirm or correct on a job, instead of nothing at all. **It does not make a quantity engine-ready without your signature** — that ceiling is fixed in the schema and this does not lift it. |
|  **If you reject the room-driven rule** |  The demotion is reversed and area density is restored as a primary method. That is a configuration change, not a rebuild: both methods are still in the model. |
|  **What it unblocks** |  Every count that reaches a valuation. **SV-S4, the report template, cannot be filled in without it.** |
|  **One thing that is open by purchase, not by judgement, and is not yours to answer** |  Data and comms outlets per unit floor area. The figure lives in structured-cabling design standards we do not hold and have not licensed. **We are deliberately not modelling around it**, because reasoning our way to a different number when a published answer exists is how a project acquires an invented figure that later looks sourced. Every row depending on it currently resolves to "unresolved" and one fixture line is simply missing from totals. **That is a visible, labelled gap and it is the correct behaviour.** The decision is whether to buy the standard, and it is ours, not yours. |

## SV-S3 · Depreciation — two methods built, neither chosen

|  **What it is** |  How a replacement-cost figure is reduced for the age and condition of what is actually there. There are two defensible methods and they are not the same shape: **per element** — each item of plant depreciated over its own expected life, so a lift and a shell get different factors — or **whole building** — one factor derived from the building's age, applied to everything. |
| --- | --- |
|  **What state it is in** |  **Both methods fully built and tested. Both switched off, with empty tables. The default is "no depreciation at all", which is exactly what the system did before either was built, so no number anywhere has changed.** |
|  **Why the default is "none" rather than one of the two methods** |  **A default is not neutral. It is what runs when nobody decides, and on the day a table lands, the default is what silently starts applying.** Defaulting to a method would be choosing between them by inaction — and choosing between them is precisely what we are handing to you. |
|  **Which way the money moves** |  On a worked case — a 30-year-old building with a 30-year-old lift and a 30-year-old shell — **per element gives 0.25 for the lift and 0.55 for the shell; whole building gives 0.60 for both.** Qualifying figures **more than 5% apart** on that case. **We deliberately do not assert which is higher in general**: it depends entirely on the age profile of the plant against the age of the building, and that dependence is exactly why you have to choose rather than us. |
|  **What we are asking you to do — and the first question is the cheap one** |  **(a) May a whole-building obsolescence scale be applied per plant element at all?** Our per-element method applies a whole-BUILDING obsolescence scale to individual plant items over a published plant life. **That is a methodological hybrid nobody has ruled on. It may be defensible. It is an invention, and it has never been asked.** **If the answer is no, the per-element method does not ship and the whole-building method is what we use.** It is a cheap question against an expensive assumption, which is why we ask it before buying either data licence. **(b) Per element or whole building — and does it depend on the case?** More accurate and much more work, versus quick and cruder. **We do not know whether the extra accuracy is worth having or is false precision.** If it depends, tell us on what and we will build that as the selector. **(c)** Where should expected plant lives come from? We assumed a published indicative-life guide. **Is a published indicative life the right input at all for an asset whose condition you can see?** **(d)** What happens past the end of a scale? We clamp to the last band and apply a residual floor. **Is a floor the right instrument for an asset past its indicative life but plainly still in service?** **(e)** Does the pool split legitimately bear on the choice? Depreciating at one rate moves value between rate bands. **Is that a tax consideration with no business influencing a valuation judgement? We suspect the latter and would rather be told.** |
|  **If you confirm one method** |  We set the switch to it, load the one table it needs, and it is live. **The other method stays built and unused, which costs nothing.** |
|  **If you reject both** |  The default already is "no depreciation", so nothing has to be undone. |
|  **What it unblocks** |  Whether we buy a data licence, and which one. **Answer (a) and (b) before we pursue either in earnest: if (a) comes back negative, neither licence was ever the real blocker for the method we end up using.** |
|  **What we have guarded against, so you do not have to check it** |  A switch on over an empty table **refuses rather than guesses**. A missing age **refuses rather than assuming nought**. A scale we do not hold **refuses rather than falling back to a different one**. Past the end of a scale we **clamp rather than extrapolate**, because extrapolating a scale beyond its last band is inventing data. **A working asset never depreciates to zero** — the residual floor is read from the scale, never assumed. And applying both methods at once is an **error**, not a warning, because depreciating the same asset twice is never right. |
